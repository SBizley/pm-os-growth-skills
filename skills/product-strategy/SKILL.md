# Skill: Product Strategy System

## Purpose
Produce a product strategy that's evidenced, coherent, and actually
usable to make prioritisation calls — through a structured, four-phase
process with a human checkpoint between each phase. Splitting context
gathering from research from writing from review stops the most common
failure mode: a strategy doc that's really just a features wishlist
with a vision statement bolted on top.

## When to use
- Setting or refreshing strategy for a product area, product line, or
  new bet
- Onboarding into a product area and needing to build the strategic
  picture from scratch
- A roadmap keeps getting re-litigated because there's no shared,
  written strategy to point back to
- Company priorities shifted and existing strategy needs re-testing
  against the new context

## How it works
Four sequential skills, each producing a distinct artifact, with a
human review checkpoint between every phase. Don't skip a checkpoint
to save time — the checkpoints are what stop bad context or weak
research from silently propagating into the final strategy.

```
1. Gather Context  →  [checkpoint]  →  2. Research  →  [checkpoint]
        →  3. Write  →  [checkpoint]  →  4. Review  →  [sign-off]
```

**Before starting:** read the "How to Write a Product Strategy" guide
once (below), then duplicate the Strategy Workspace template for the
specific product area — one workspace per strategy effort.

## Phase 1 — Gather Context

**Purpose:** establish the factual ground the strategy will be built
on before anyone starts forming opinions about direction.

**Inputs to pull together:**
- Current product/business performance (revenue, growth, retention,
  usage — whatever's the relevant top-line metric for this area)
- Company-level strategic priorities and constraints this product
  strategy needs to nest inside
- Competitive landscape — who else is playing here and how
- Prior strategy docs for this area, if any — what was decided last
  time and whether it's still true
- Known internal constraints: budget, team size, technical debt,
  regulatory environment

**Output:** a context brief — facts and current-state summary only, no
direction or opinions yet. This is deliberately boring; save the
thinking for phase 3.

**Checkpoint:** a human reviews the context brief for accuracy and
completeness before research starts. Wrong or missing context here
invalidates everything downstream.

## Phase 2 — Research

**Purpose:** fill the specific gaps the context brief exposed —
market, customer, and competitive research, not a generic literature
review.

**Approach:**
- Let the context brief drive the research questions — research what's
  actually uncertain, not everything theoretically knowable
- Mix external research (market sizing, competitor moves, industry
  trends) with internal research (customer interviews, usage data,
  support/sales signal) — see the Discovery and Market Sizing skills
  for the methods
- Distinguish sourced findings from your own inference at every step

**Output:** a research findings doc, organized by the question each
finding answers — not a dump of everything found.

**Checkpoint:** a human reviews the research for gaps, source quality,
and whether it actually answers the open questions from phase 1.

## Phase 3 — Write

**Purpose:** turn context + research into an actual strategy — a
specific, falsifiable point of view on where to play and how to win,
not a restatement of the research.

**A strategy document should answer, explicitly:**
- **Where to play** — which customers, segments, and problems this
  product will (and won't) serve
- **How to win** — the specific advantage or differentiation this
  relies on, and why it's defensible
- **What capabilities it requires** — what has to be true internally
  (team, tech, partnerships) for the "how to win" to hold
- **Strategic pillars** — the 2-4 themes that translate the strategy
  into roadmap-shaping priorities
- **Success metrics** — how you'll know if the strategy is working,
  with targets and a timeframe
- **What's explicitly out of scope** — as important as what's in

**Output:** the strategy document, following the shared workspace
template so every strategy in the system reads consistently.

**Checkpoint:** a human reviews the draft for whether it's actually a
strategy (a specific bet with trade-offs) rather than a list of good
intentions everyone can agree with.

## Phase 4 — Review

**Purpose:** a structured critique pass before the strategy ships,
checking it against failure patterns rather than just "does this sound
good."

**Review against:**
- **Falsifiable** — could this strategy be wrong? A strategy that's
  unfalsifiable isn't a strategy
- **Evidenced** — does every major claim trace back to something in
  the research, or is it asserted?
- **Coherent** — do the pillars actually follow from "how to win," or
  are they a grab-bag of good ideas?
- **Resourced** — are the required capabilities realistic given actual
  team/budget constraints, or does it assume a team you don't have?
- **Differentiated** — would a competitor's strategy doc, with the
  names swapped, read basically the same? If so, it's not
  differentiated enough
- **Excludes something** — a strategy that says yes to everything
  isn't making real trade-offs

**Output:** a review readout — what passed, what needs rework, and
whether it's ready for final sign-off.

**Checkpoint:** final sign-off from whoever owns the strategy decision.

## Quality checklist (whole system)
- [ ] Context brief is fact-only, reviewed before research began
- [ ] Research answers the specific open questions from context, not generic
- [ ] Strategy states where to play AND where it won't
- [ ] "How to win" is specific enough to be wrong
- [ ] Strategic pillars trace directly to the "how to win"
- [ ] Success metrics have targets and a timeframe
- [ ] The review pass happened as a distinct step, not folded into writing
- [ ] Something was explicitly excluded or deprioritised
